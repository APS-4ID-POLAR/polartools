polartools.spectrum_fit#

Gaussian peak fitting for the spectrum viewer’s trend.

The trend in spectrum_viewer totals a box ROI per frame. This module offers the alternative of fitting several Gaussians and a background over a window of bins, and taking the area of the chosen peaks instead – for lines that overlap, or sit on a background a box cannot take out.

The shape is fitted once, the areas are a projection. The centres and widths are fitted (with lmfit) to a summed spectrum – many frames, both halves of a contrast – and then held fixed. With the shape fixed, the least-squares areas of any spectrum y over the same window are linear in y: areas = pinv(G) @ y, where G holds one unit-area Gaussian per peak and the background’s columns. area_weights() returns the row of pinv(G) for the peaks wanted, so that one frame’s fitted area is a dot product with its spectrum.

That linearity is the whole design. The area of a sum of frames is the sum of the frames’ areas, exactly as for an ROI sum, so the viewer can compute a per-frame area and hand it to the same machinery that totals ROI counts over points, halves and monitor-normalised ratios. A free fit at every point would follow a line that moves, and would lose that: the halves and the monitors could no longer share the reduction, and it is three orders of magnitude slower. Measured on a 2026-3 dichro scan’s Pt line, the fixed-shape areas agree with free per-frame fits to 0.09 % (median), and cost 0.07 ms for 312 frames against 140 ms. Shape-from-the-sum is also the physics a contrast wants: polarization changes intensities, not the detector’s line shape. It is the wrong tool for the elastic and Compton lines of an energy scan, which move with the incident energy.

The background costs noise. Its columns are estimated from the wings of the window, which hold few counts. On that same Pt line, an isolated one, the point-to-point noise of the XMCD/XAS ratio is 9.0e-3 from an ROI sum, 9.9e-3 from a fitted area with no background, and 1.26e-2 with a constant or linear one. Fitting pays where peaks overlap or the background slopes; on a clean line a box ROI is as good.

The least-squares projection is unweighted on purpose. Poisson weights would be better per spectrum, but they depend on y, and the projection then stops being linear – the property everything above rests on. The shape fit itself is Poisson-weighted, since it is done once on a spectrum of many counts.

No Qt here, so that all of it can be tested on synthetic spectra.

polartools.spectrum_fit.fit_shape(y: numpy.ndarray, lo: int, hi: int, peaks: list[PeakSpec], background: str = 'linear', shared_width: bool = False) → ShapeFit[source]#

Fit centres, widths and areas of peaks to y[lo:hi + 1].

y is a whole spectrum, indexed by bin. Poisson-weighted, with the centres kept inside the window and near where they started (CENTRE_SLACK_SIGMAS), and, with shared_width, every width tied to the first – the detector’s resolution changes little across a few hundred eV. Never raises: a failure comes back as success False.

polartools.spectrum_fit.area_weights(shape: ShapeFit, used) → numpy.ndarray[source]#

Weights over bins lo..hi whose dot product with a spectrum is its area.

The summed area of the peaks indexed by used, solved by unweighted least squares with shape’s centres and widths held fixed. Linear in the spectrum, so totals over frames commute with it – see the module docstring. Zeros when nothing is used.

polartools.spectrum_fit.decompose(y: numpy.ndarray, shape: ShapeFit) → tuple[numpy.ndarray, numpy.ndarray][source]#

(coefficients, matrix): y’s fixed-shape least-squares solve.

The same projection the trend uses, so the curves drawn from it and the areas listed beside them are the numbers the trend sums. Coefficients are the peaks’ areas first, then the background’s.

polartools.spectrum_fit.suggest_peaks(y: numpy.ndarray, lo: int, hi: int, max_peaks: int = 6, prominence: float = 0.05, smooth: int = 7) → list[PeakSpec][source]#

Starting peaks for y[lo:hi + 1], the most prominent first.

Found as peaks of the curvature -y'' rather than of y, because the lines worth fitting are the ones that overlap, and a weak line on a strong one’s tail is a shoulder, not a maximum: find_peaks on the counts misses it outright. Every Gaussian, shoulder or not, is a maximum of -y''. The derivative is taken through a Savitzky-Golay filter smooth bins wide, since a raw second difference of counts is mostly noise.

prominence is a fraction of the largest curvature in the window, and a candidate narrower than MIN_RELATIVE_WIDTH of the most prominent peak is dropped as a ripple. A Gaussian’s curvature changes sign at one sigma either side of its centre, so half the run over which -y'' stays positive is the starting width – overlapping lines narrow it, and the fit corrects it.
